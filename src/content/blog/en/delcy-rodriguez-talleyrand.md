On 22 September 2026, a photograph was taken in New York that would have been hard to imagine a few months earlier.




Delcy Rodríguez stood smiling next to Donald Trump. She later described the encounter as “historic” and said they had discussed strengthening relations between Venezuela and the United States and cooperation in areas such as energy, mining and security. It was not a formal bilateral summit, but a meeting during a reception hosted by Trump on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly. Even so, it was the first time the two had met in person since the capture of Nicolás Maduro in January.




The contrast was striking.




As late as 12 February, more than a month after Maduro’s capture, Rodríguez was still publicly maintaining that he remained the “legitimate president” of Venezuela and defending both his innocence and that of Cilia Flores. At the same time, she was already signalling a willingness to cooperate with the United States, especially on energy.




Seven months later she was in New York meeting the American president under whose administration Maduro had been captured.




The transformation should not be exaggerated. In Venezuela, chavismo did not vanish to be replaced by an entirely opposite regime. Rodríguez came from within that same power structure and assumed the presidency from within it. What she has managed to survive so far is, above all, **the fall of the man she had served**.




But the photograph recalls a much older political skill: making sure that the disappearance of the power one seemed to depend on does not also mean one’s own disappearance.




And few carried that skill as far as Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord.




Talleyrand was born under Louis XV. He was a bishop under Louis XVI, took part in the French Revolution, served as a minister of the Directory, helped Napoleon Bonaparte rise, served the Consulate and the Empire, later manoeuvred against Napoleon, contributed to the Bourbon Restoration and, when another revolution drove the Bourbons out again, ended up working for Louis-Philippe.




Between his birth in 1754 and his death in 1838, France went from the monarchy of the Ancien Régime to a constitutional monarchy; then to the Republic, the Directory, the Consulate, the Empire, the Bourbon Restoration, the Hundred Days, a second Restoration and finally the July Monarchy.




Talleyrand passed through almost all of those political worlds.

## A bishop in the middle of a revolution




Had anyone tried in 1788 to guess which Frenchmen were best placed to survive politically over the following decades, Talleyrand would not have looked like the safest bet.




He came from an old aristocratic family and had just become Bishop of Autun. By birth and by profession he belonged precisely to the two estates whose world was about to be radically transformed.




Yet when the Ancien Régime began to crumble, Talleyrand did not cling to it.




Elected as a deputy for the clergy to the Estates-General, he moved towards the reformers and played an important part in the break between the new France and the old ecclesiastical order. He argued that the Church’s property should be placed at the nation’s disposal and took part in the revolutionary reorganisation of the clergy.




The aristocrat and bishop had found a place within the Revolution that was dismantling the world that had produced him.




He did not, however, follow the Revolution through the whole of its radicalisation. In 1792 he left France, spending time in Britain and then in the United States. By the time he returned, the most violent phase of the Revolution was over.




By 1797 he was Foreign Minister of the Directory.




Less than ten years had passed since he had been a bishop under Louis XVI.




## When the future had a name: Bonaparte




Nor did Talleyrand tie his fate to the Directory.




By the late 1790s that regime was weakened, and one general was becoming ever more prominent: Napoleon Bonaparte. Talleyrand took part in the manoeuvres surrounding the coup of 18 Brumaire in 1799 and soon returned to the Foreign Ministry.




First he served Bonaparte as consul. Then Napoleon as emperor.




At this point it is tempting to explain the man simply: Talleyrand was extraordinarily good at spotting who was going to win.




And there are good reasons not to idealise him.




His reputation for corruption does not come only from his enemies. During the so-called **XYZ Affair**, American diplomats sent to negotiate with France were approached by intermediaries who passed on a series of demands. Among them was the payment of a substantial personal bribe to Talleyrand. The incident helped trigger the so-called Quasi-War between France and the United States.




We are not, then, dealing with a political saint.




But the simple explanation that he was merely an opportunist begins to run into trouble when we look at what happened next.




Because there came a moment when the man who held almost all the power was Napoleon.




And Talleyrand began to work against him.




## What if Talleyrand changed less than it seems?




This is where his story becomes much more interesting.




Behind all those changes of regime lay a certain continuity in the way Talleyrand thought about foreign policy.




He saw Europe essentially as a system of balances. France should be powerful, but trying to impose permanent hegemony through conquest would end up driving the other great powers to unite against her.




And that idea did not appear after the fact, once Napoleon began losing wars.




In a memorandum written as early as **1792**, Talleyrand argued that France should concentrate on perfecting her own institutions rather than on continually extending her borders. He was wary of a policy of annexations and believed that European stability required some kind of balance between states.




At first, serving Bonaparte was not necessarily incompatible with that vision. The Consulate seemed capable of stabilising France, consolidating some of the Revolution’s gains and negotiating from a position of strength.




The problem, for Talleyrand, was that Napoleon did not stop.




After Austerlitz their disagreements became more visible. Talleyrand wanted to avoid weakening Austria too much, sought a rapprochement with Britain and distrusted an open-ended French expansion. After Tilsit, in 1807, he left the Foreign Ministry, although Napoleon kept him close and continued to seek his advice.




Up to this point one could build a fairly favourable reading of his conduct: Talleyrand served Napoleon while he believed it benefited France, and parted ways with him when he concluded that his policy was leading the country towards an unsustainable hegemony.




But then came Erfurt.




## Betraying Napoleon to save France

</figure>




In 1808 Napoleon met Tsar Alexander I of Russia at Erfurt.




The situation was delicate. He needed to keep Russia cooperating while he tried to deal with the war in Spain and hold Austria in check. For such an important negotiation he turned to Talleyrand once more.




And Talleyrand used the occasion to work against the emperor’s own strategy.




He kept up contacts with Alexander and encouraged him to resist Napoleon’s demands. The political break that had been building for years was thus exposed in a way that was hard to disguise: a former French minister was advising a foreign sovereign to stand up to the man he was still accompanying as an adviser.




It is hard to describe this without the word **betrayal** appearing in some sense.




Talleyrand’s defence was different. If Napoleon was leading France to disaster, one could argue that working against Napoleon meant being loyal to something higher than the emperor: France.




The problem is obvious.




Talleyrand reserved for himself the right to decide when it was no longer legitimate to obey the ruler he served.




And that prerogative had one extraordinary advantage: it allowed France’s salvation and Talleyrand’s own survival to coincide remarkably often.




Therein lies much of the enigma.




It is entirely possible that he sincerely believed in a particular idea of French interests and, at the same time, that he had an exceptional instinct for abandoning a ship before others realised it was starting to sink.




Both things can be true.




## He was not the only one




Talleyrand had a contemporary who took political survival down a different path: Joseph Fouché.




Fouché had been a revolutionary, a regicide and then Napoleon’s powerful Minister of Police. He held that ministry several times and passed through almost every regime of the age, systematically cultivating alternative channels in case circumstances changed. After Waterloo he even negotiated with the Bourbons and briefly returned as Minister of Police before ending up dismissed and exiled.




In 1808 Talleyrand and Fouché, who deeply distrusted each other, began to draw closer while Napoleon was in Spain. The emperor saw these manoeuvres as a threat and rushed back to Paris, furious with both.




There is a small but useful distinction here.




Talleyrand represents above all the man capable of passing through different political systems while justifying his shifts by a particular idea of the national interest.




Fouché better represents the man who stays inside the apparatus while preparing to outlast whoever happens, for the moment, to sit at its top.




In that particular respect, there may be something of Fouché, as well as of Talleyrand, in the image of Delcy Rodríguez that catches our attention today.




What, then, was Talleyrand’s method of survival? It was not simply a matter of making himself indispensable to each new ruler. It was a harder combination to reproduce: sensing before others where power was shifting, avoiding identifying himself entirely with the fortunes of a single man, and keeping something the next regime might need.




In Talleyrand’s case, that asset was extraordinary: he knew European diplomacy, understood the interests of the other powers and knew how to turn that knowledge into results. Napoleon kept turning to him even after he had left the ministry, and in 1814 Talleyrand saw that the moment had come to prepare the return of the Bourbons.




Above all, he had few qualms about starting to prepare the future while the present still seemed solid. He did not always wait for a regime to fall before changing position. Much of his talent lay precisely in **moving first**.




## When Napoleon fell, Talleyrand was ready

  <img src="/blog/images/delcy-rodriguez-talleyrand-congreso-viena.png" alt="The plenipotentiaries of the Congress of Vienna, engraving after Jean-Baptiste Isabey" loading="lazy" />

</figure>




In 1814 the allied powers entered France.




Napoleon was defeated and Talleyrand returned to the centre of events. He headed the provisional government and played an active part in restoring Louis XVIII. The former bishop who had worked with the Revolution that drove out the Bourbons was now helping to put them back on the throne.




But his greatest display of diplomatic skill was yet to come.




France had just lost more than twenty years of revolutionary and Napoleonic wars. Russia, Prussia, Austria and Britain were the great victorious powers, and they met in Vienna to reorganise Europe.




Talleyrand arrived representing defeated France.




His problem was to achieve something that seemed contradictory: for France to stop being treated as the vanquished enemy and to be regarded once again as one of the powers needed to maintain the continental balance.




The opportunity lay in the victors’ own divisions.




Russia wanted to greatly expand its influence over Poland. Prussia aspired to annex a large part of Saxony. Austria and Britain watched the growth of both with unease.




Talleyrand slipped into that rift by defending the principle of legitimacy and the rights of the King of Saxony. The argument had a legal and political dimension, but it fitted French interests perfectly: getting the allies to stop looking only at France and to start distrusting one another.




The manoeuvre helped bring France closer to Austria and Britain against Russia and Prussia. The former enemy was thus back in the European diplomatic game far sooner than its recent defeat would have suggested.




There is no need to turn Talleyrand into a puppet master capable of controlling the Congress of Vienna single-handed. The other powers had their own interests, and circumstances made the manoeuvre possible.




But that was precisely one of his great skills: understanding what others needed and getting them, suddenly, to need France as well.




## He also knew what it was to lose




Talleyrand’s career was not, however, an unbroken series of victories.




After Waterloo he briefly headed the government again and returned to the Foreign Ministry, but his relations with Louis XVIII and the Bourbon circle deteriorated. In September 1815 he left power.




For many years he remained on the sidelines of political life. He kept his social standing, remained a well-known and influential figure and followed events closely, but he no longer directed French policy.




It is an important correction to his legend.




Talleyrand knew how to survive. That did not mean he always won.




His last great opportunity came with another revolution.




In 1830 Charles X was overthrown and the senior branch of the Bourbons lost the throne once more. Louis-Philippe became king and called on the elderly Talleyrand for one last great mission: to represent France in London and help bring about a rapprochement with Britain and a resolution of the Belgian crisis.




The former bishop of the Ancien Régime ended his diplomatic career working for a monarchy born of yet another revolution.




But one last reconciliation still awaited him.




## The final return




Talleyrand had begun his adult life as a priest and a bishop.




During the Revolution he took part in decisions that led to his break with Rome. He supported the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, took part in consecrating constitutional bishops and later, in practice, abandoned the ecclesiastical life and married.




Almost half a century later, as his life drew to a close, the question of his relationship with the Church resurfaced.




And what happened is more interesting than the simple story of a last-minute deathbed conversion.




Documents from the circle of the Archbishop of Paris show that the terms of his reconciliation were discussed for weeks. The Church demanded a declaration of submission and repentance that referred specifically to some of the acts of his life that **had brought him into conflict with it**. Two drafts prepared by Talleyrand himself even survive, from before the text was amended by the archbishop.




On 17 May 1838, the day of his death, Talleyrand finally signed the declaration needed to complete that reconciliation and receive the sacraments.




Was he sincere? Was it the last precaution of a man who had spent his whole life making sure no exit was ever closed? How far can a historian know what goes on in the conscience of someone who knows he is about to die?




We cannot settle those questions.




But, from a Christian perspective, there is an irony that is hard to ignore. After a lifetime of outlasting kings, revolutions and emperors, Talleyrand finally found himself before an authority that no longer depended on who governed France.




**And, this time, before a power he could not outlast.**




## So was he simply a turncoat?




That question is probably too simple.




Talleyrand was an opportunist. He took money, engaged in intrigue, worked against people he officially served and showed an extraordinary ability to be close to the next power when the previous one was beginning to sink.




But neither does he seem to have been an entirely empty vessel, ready to adopt whatever idea was needed to keep a post.




Long before Napoleon one can already find in him a relatively stable conception of international politics: distrust of unlimited territorial expansion, a preference for balance among the great powers and the conviction that France would be safer within a stable European system than trying to dominate it permanently.




That is why Talleyrand is far more interesting than a mere traitor.




He forces us to ask what loyalty really means for a statesman.




Should he be loyal to the ruler he serves? To the regime that gave him his post? To certain institutions? To a set of ideas? To his country?




Can someone work against his own ruler and claim, at the same time, that by doing so he is being more loyal to the country than that ruler is?




And, above all, who decides when a higher loyalty allows a lower one to be broken?




Talleyrand’s answer had one rather convenient feature:




Talleyrand decided.




## Back to Delcy

Two centuries later we can return to that photograph in New York.




Allowing for the enormous historical distance, Talleyrand’s life lets us look differently at one sentence from Rodríguez.




In February she was still insisting that Nicolás Maduro was Venezuela’s legitimate president. In September, after meeting Trump, she said she would keep pursuing **“diplomacy that serves the national interest”**, aimed at achieving concrete results for Venezuela.




What happens when those two loyalties stop pointing in the same direction?




Has the politician’s loyalty changed?




Or has that politician’s understanding of what deserves their loyalty changed?




There is also something very concrete that helps explain why Washington and Caracas may now find each other useful. During Rodríguez’s trip to New York, the Venezuelan delegation held talks with American and international counterparts on energy, mining, debt and financing, against a backdrop of growing economic cooperation between the two countries.




That gives the photograph another dimension.




We do not know how Delcy Rodríguez’s story will end. Nor do we know whether, a few decades from now, the comparison with the old French diplomat will look perceptive or merely clever.




We can observe Talleyrand from a distance of two centuries. We know what he did when the monarchy fell, what he did when the Directory disappeared, what he did when Napoleon rose, what he did when he came to think Napoleon was leading France to disaster, and what he did when the emperor finally fell.




With Rodríguez, we are seeing just one of those moments as it happens.




But that photograph recalls something Talleyrand understood extraordinarily well.




Powerful men can seem indispensable. Regimes can seem eternal and alliances unbreakable.




Until they are not.




When that happens, some politicians disappear along with the power that made them.




Others manage to convince the next power that it, too, may need them.




Perhaps Talleyrand’s secret was not that he lacked loyalties.




**Perhaps it lay in always reserving for himself the right to decide what he owed loyalty to.**
