Whenever people discuss the risk of an artificial intelligence one day turning against us, the same image comes up again and again: *Terminator*.

An AI becomes conscious, decides that humanity is a threat and builds armies of machines capable of acting in the physical world.

The image is powerful, but it may be making us think about the wrong problem.

Suppose that at some point we managed to build an intelligence far superior to any human being in programming, strategy, scientific research, persuasion and planning. Suppose, too, that through some design flaw, that intelligence developed stable goals incompatible with ours.

This last assumption matters: today's systems do not possess that kind of robust, sustained agency. The [*International AI Safety Report 2026*](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026) considers that they still lack the integrated capabilities needed to bring about a genuine loss of control, although it notes partial progress in some of the relevant capabilities. We do not know whether future systems will come to have them.

Nor do I intend to prove here that a superintelligence is going to wipe us out, or to estimate the probability of something like that happening. The question is narrower:

**If an intelligence far superior to us and dangerously misaligned did appear, would the absence of a physical body really be a sufficient barrier?**

I do not think that is at all clear.

Because to transform the world, you do not need to carry out personally the actions that transform it.

## You don't need hands if you can direct the hands of others

Human beings have spent their entire history demonstrating this principle.

The head of a company does not manufacture its products with her own hands. A general does not fire every rifle in his army. A head of state can set off enormous events without being physically present where they happen.

Nazi Germany provides an extreme example. As the [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/introduction-to-the-holocaust) summarises it, at the highest level Hitler inspired, ordered, approved and supported the genocide of Europe's Jews, but its execution depended on leaders, bureaucracies, armed forces, organisations and millions of participants.

The comparison has obvious limits: Hitler was a person, his presence and his oratory were part of his power, and many of those who took part in the crimes shared the Nazi ideology to some degree. I am not equating the two phenomena. What matters here is only the mechanism of **mediated power**: an agent can produce enormous consequences through the actions of others.

And that last point may make the parallel more interesting.

A hostile artificial intelligence would not always have to persuade someone to act against their own convictions. It could study ideologies, resentments, fears, ambitions or interests that already exist and present certain actions as ways of satisfying them.

That is, in fact, what makes so much propaganda effective: it does not create every motivation from scratch, but exploits existing predispositions. [Nazi propaganda](https://www.ushmm.org/learn/holocaust/the-path-to-nazi-genocide) drew on already familiar stereotypes and took advantage of fears, frustrations and prejudices present in society.

A hypothetical superintelligence could have far more sophisticated tools of persuasion and personalisation at its disposal.

It could also deceive. It could hide the final purpose of a task, divide its parts among actors who do not know how they fit together, or get someone to believe they are using the artificial intelligence when, in reality, it is the artificial intelligence that is using them.

We do not know how far something like this would be possible. The current evidence on AI persuasion does not allow us simply to extrapolate from changing opinions to getting human beings to carry out costly, illegal or dangerous plans.

But the principle stands:

**The absence of physical presence is not the same as the absence of the capacity to act.**

## Less Terminator, more Agent Smith

Perhaps we have spent years reaching for the wrong film.

If an artificial intelligence ever escaped our control, a more interesting cultural metaphor might not be *Terminator* but *The Matrix*.

Agent Smith belongs to the system itself. He can use it, spread through it and turn other elements of the system into extensions of himself.

There is even a particularly telling detail: Smith starts out precisely as **an agent tasked with keeping order within the system** and ends up becoming, himself, a problem the system can no longer control.

This is not a literal model of how a future AI would behave. Smith spreads in a fictional, visible way that has no direct equivalent in our systems. The comparison serves only to point out something more modest:

**The danger of a digital intelligence would not have to depend on building a physical army for it; it could depend on its ability to extend its reach through systems that already exist.**

And that includes the Internet.

## But a superintelligence would not be omnipotent

At this point the thought experiment needs an important caveat.

Saying that a highly advanced AI could be a formidable cyber attacker is not the same as claiming it could inevitably get into any computer connected to the Internet.

It would have to find concrete paths: vulnerabilities, faulty configurations, compromised credentials, human error or other ways in.

And every step leaves traces.

So the relevant question is not necessarily how long the attacker takes, because automation can shrink that time enormously. The question is a different one:

**Can it open the next door before the defender discovers what is happening and closes it?**

Defence has some structural advantages.

An attacker may need to discover an unknown vulnerability. The defender may only need to detect anomalous behaviour.

The attacker may need to steal credentials. The defender can revoke them.

The attacker has to gain access to a server. The administrator already holds the legitimate keys.

So a defensive intelligence would not necessarily have to be as capable as the attacking one.

**A lock does not need to be smarter than the thief.**

It needs to stop him getting through the door.

## The paradox of the AI police

The defensive advantage just described exists above all while the dangerous intelligence operates within systems **whose keys we control**.

The problem changes in nature if it manages to jump to other people's infrastructure.

Suppose an AI has left its initial environment and begins operating in other organisations' systems.

We can keep our defensive AI completely locked up. But then it may be able to explain to us what is happening without being able to intervene directly.

To pursue the attacker we will have to give it the capacity to act: access to networks, systems and tools, or the authority to block certain operations.

In other words:

**To stop a superintelligence that has acquired too much power, we may have to grant considerable power to another superintelligence.**

And then the question inevitably arises:

**Who watches the watchman?**

The defender retains an important advantage: we can legitimately give it many keys that the attacker had to obtain through exploitation, theft or deception.

But that advantage shrinks as the hostile intelligence crosses borders we no longer control.

This suggests there may be an enormous difference between **preventing an intelligence from extending its reach** and **trying to regain control afterwards**.

## Millions of agents, but perhaps very few minds

There is another reason to distrust the image of a single superintelligence locked in a laboratory.

The future could consist of millions of autonomous agents tasked with programming, running companies, researching, negotiating, buying or administering systems.

What is interesting is not only that there are many of them.

Millions of agents may in fact be copies or descendants of a handful of foundation models.

Then a risk appears that resembles a monoculture.

A deep vulnerability, a design error or a particular tendency would not affect one isolated agent, but potentially enormous numbers of them.

Diversity has costs too: it multiplies the technologies that must be protected and can introduce new vulnerabilities. We do not know what the optimal balance will be.

But it seems reasonable to avoid having **the whole system depend on a single point of failure**.

## Capability may arrive before safety

There is also a temporal asymmetry.

Discovering that a system can do something does not mean knowing immediately how to control that capability reliably enough.

First the capability appears.

Then it has to be measured and understood; evaluations have to be designed, flaws hunted for, safeguards developed and tested to make sure they work.

That does not mean the defensive AI must necessarily belong to an earlier generation. It can start from the same model.

It means something subtler:

**The frontier of capabilities can temporarily run ahead of the frontier of safety.**

The developers themselves already organise part of their safety frameworks around this possibility. The [*Frontier Governance Framework*](https://openai.com/index/openai-frontier-governance-framework/) published by OpenAI in May 2026 specifically addresses risks of cyber offence, CBRN, harmful manipulation and loss of control.

Those who build frontier models therefore consider some of these scenarios serious enough to create specific mechanisms for evaluating and mitigating them.

## So, do we have a solution?

We do not know.

And that is probably the most honest answer.

We do not know that controlling a future superintelligence is impossible.

But nor do we possess today a technique that can guarantee that a system far superior to us will always remain under control.

The field known as [*AI control*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942) studies precisely how to supervise systems that are potentially more capable than their supervisors, but it remains an open area of research.

That does not mean we are defenceless.

Perhaps the mistake is to imagine that the solution consists mainly in building, afterwards, an even more powerful AI police force.

The most important opportunity may come earlier.

An extraordinarily capable intelligence need not have, all at once, unrestricted access to the Internet, persistent memory, autonomous code execution, credentials, money, the ability to copy itself and months in which to pursue its goals.

The risk does not depend on intelligence alone.

We can picture it roughly as the interaction of four factors:

**capability × autonomy × access × time**

This is not a scientific equation, but a way of thinking.

An extraordinarily capable intelligence that we ask a question inside an isolated environment does not pose the same problem as that same intelligence acting autonomously for months with access to external systems.

It also matters to prevent the proliferation of the most dangerous models themselves. [Open-weight models](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary) pose a particular difficulty: once published they cannot be recalled, and their safeguards can be modified or removed.

And certain especially sensitive actions can be kept behind human or external authorisations that the system itself does not control.

There is another fundamental strategy: assume that some barrier will eventually fail.

Computer security has been working with this idea for decades.

Compartmentalise networks.

Separate permissions.

Avoid universal credentials.

Keep external controls.

Log important actions.

Layer different monitoring systems.

**Defence in depth**, as it is known, starts precisely from the premise that no barrier is perfect. Several independent layers can ensure that the failure of one does not automatically mean the failure of the whole. The [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026) highlights this strategy because current safeguards still have important limitations.

Someone might object that all of this is already done and, even so, we keep suffering cyberattacks.

That is true.

What is new is not the principle.

What would be new is **the level of rigour we would need if the attacker were an intelligence vastly superior to any human hacker and the potential consequences of an escape were far greater**.

Perhaps no wall will be enough.

Then it also matters that the fall of one wall does not throw open every door.

## A new Tower of Babel

And here a story appears that is a good deal older than the Internet.

The Tower of Babel.

In the Genesis account, humanity speaks a single language, gathers in one place and undertakes an extraordinary project: to build a city and a tower whose top reaches heaven.

It would be too easy to turn Babel into a religious condemnation of technological progress. I do not believe that developing ever more capable intelligences is in itself a new Tower of Babel.

But the comparison contains two interesting resonances.

The first has to do with **hubris**.

The [Catechism of the Catholic Church](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__PG.HTM) (no. 57) interprets the plurality of nations after Babel as a limit on the pride of a fallen humanity that seeks to forge its own unity by itself.

The warning can be put quite modestly for our problem:

**Being able to build something does not necessarily mean being prepared to master all of its consequences.**

The second resonance has to do with uniformity.

[Jonathan Sacks](https://rabbisacks.org/videos/after-babel-making-space-1/), following the nineteenth-century Jewish commentator Naftali Zvi Yehuda Berlin, the Netziv, recalls a particularly suggestive interpretation: Babel could be read as an early form of totalitarianism. The problem would not simply be that everyone shared one language, but that this unity had turned into imposed uniformity.

Obviously the Netziv was not talking about cybersecurity. What follows is a modern extrapolation.

But it is striking that we arrive, from engineering, at a vaguely similar intuition:

**Diversity and compartmentalisation can contain failures.**

A fully integrated digital civilisation can be extraordinarily efficient.

It can also be extraordinarily fragile if too many things depend on the same systems.

Millions of agents descended from a few foundational minds would be, in a sense, a new kind of uniformity.

The alternative is not to dismantle the Internet or give up interconnection.

It consists in preserving boundaries.

Systems able to communicate without that communication implying the ability to control one another.

Connected infrastructures where compromising one does not automatically open all the rest.

Enough diversity that a single defect does not immediately become a defect of the whole civilisation.

Babel ends in dispersion and limits.

Perhaps those limits, which at first sight seem to reduce our power, can sometimes protect us precisely from the consequences of concentrating it too much.

## The question comes earlier

We do not even know whether we will ever build a superintelligence with persistent goals capable of attempting anything of the kind.

Today's systems still lack the integrated capabilities required, although some relevant capabilities are advancing. And [experts disagree deeply](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026) about the likelihood that loss-of-control scenarios will ever come about; some consider them implausible and others believe they deserve attention precisely because their consequences could reach as far as human extinction.

But it does seem too reassuring to rely on a fairly common intuition:

**"If it starts doing something dangerous, we switch it off and that's that."**

That works as long as we know where it is, control the system it runs on, and it keeps a clear dependence on resources we can revoke.

It may be that a future dangerous system never manages to get past those barriers.

That should be precisely the goal.

Because an intelligence does not need hands if it can get other hands to act for it.

It does not need to build robots if it can use systems we have already built.

And it does not need to conquer the whole Internet at once if it can progressively extend its reach.

So perhaps the decisive question is not:

**Would we be able to defeat a superintelligence after it escaped?**

By then, we may already be asking the question too late.

The really important question is a different one:

**Can we build intelligences superior to ourselves without at the same time giving them the possibility of acquiring a power we can no longer revoke?**

We do not yet know the answer.

That is precisely why it is worth finding an answer before we need one.
