# The problem isn't that you don't know. It's that you think you do.

*Conspiracies, know-it-all pundits and the strange prestige of having an opinion on everything.*

NATO is behind the death of a worker at a Russian institute devoted to the study of plague. After years of confrontation with Russia, Western intelligence services have realised that biological warfare can be more effective than conventional weapons. A discreet attack, hard to attribute and capable of spreading fear among the population.

**I just made that up.**

I don't have a single piece of evidence that anything of the kind has happened. What we do know is that a worker at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute, in Siberia, died on 2 October. The Russian authorities attributed her death to pneumonia of unknown origin. According to the information available as of 8 October, it had not been confirmed that she died of plague, and the World Health Organization was still requesting information.

But it took me barely a few seconds to turn a handful of real facts into a tale of international espionage. I could add a backstory of covert operations, geopolitical rivalries and statements by Western leaders. I could even explain why there is no evidence: precisely because it is a secret operation.

The more details I add, the more convincing the story can become. And yet I still won't have proved anything.

That is the problem I want to talk about.

## The difference between suspecting and knowing

Lately I find myself fairly often facing surprising claims made with a certainty that baffles me.

In a recent conversation, someone assured me that the United States and Israel had been behind the migration crisis in Ceuta at the end of July 2026, when some 72,000 people entered Spanish territory from Morocco in just two days.

I asked how they knew. And the interesting thing is that they weren't presenting a suspicion or a possibility. They insisted that they knew.

The spread of misleading messages on social media during that crisis has been investigated, and the possible involvement of foreign networks has been discussed. But spreading or amplifying disinformation is one thing; organising a mass entry of people is quite another. The former does not prove the latter, nor does it establish that the governments of the United States and Israel were behind what happened.

In another conversation, someone attributed to Anthropic, an artificial intelligence company, an alleged "Jewish bias" because, according to him, its head was Jewish. Even if that biographical premise were correct, how would it allow anyone to deduce the behaviour of the models the company develops? Where is the evidence of that bias?

What is striking is not that hypotheses are put forward. Some may be well founded and others not. What is striking is how easily certain people turn them into known facts.

That two governments share interests does not prove that they coordinated an operation. That someone might benefit from an event does not prove that they caused it. That two events coincide in time does not necessarily mean there is a causal relationship between them.

**And a story being coherent does not mean it is true.**

Of course, plausibility matters. When we try to understand something, we have to consider which explanations are reasonable. The problem arises when we mistake that initial assessment for proof.

It is one thing to say "I suspect this happened". Another to say "there are signs pointing in this direction". And quite another to say "I know this happened".

We cannot use the three expressions as if they meant the same thing.

## The pleasure of knowing what others don't

There is another element I find especially interesting: the satisfaction of believing we have discovered something that others cannot see.

We don't just find an explanation. Sometimes we enjoy the feeling of being intellectually above those who don't share it.

Other people are naive. They believe what the television tells them. They accept the official version. We, on the other hand, have managed to connect the dots.

The reward is not only understanding the world. It is also feeling sharper than everyone else.

And this intuition is backed by psychological research. In 2017, Anthony Lantian and other researchers published a study entitled *"I Know Things They Don't Know!"*. They found a relationship between belief in conspiracy theories, the feeling of possessing exclusive information and the need to feel different.

This does not mean that anyone who questions an official explanation is seeking to feel superior. But it helps us understand why certain stories can be so appealing to us even before we examine their evidence.

There is something seductive about thinking we have discovered a truth that remains hidden from most people.

The drawback is that this satisfaction can become an incentive to believe, not to investigate.

Because genuinely investigating includes the possibility of discovering that our brilliant explanation was wrong. And that is considerably less gratifying.

## Scepticism à la carte

Perhaps the most surprising contradiction appears when we observe how some people examine competing explanations.

Faced with an official version, they demand documents, testimony, conclusive evidence and explanations for every seemingly contradictory detail.

So far, no objection. It is perfectly reasonable to demand evidence.

But when an alternative explanation comes along that fits their suspicions, that rigour can vanish.

A few coincidences, a conceivable motive and the feeling that everything fits are enough.

**They demand extraordinary evidence for the first, but a chain of circumstances is enough for them for the second.**

That said, an important nuance is in order.

It is perfectly reasonable to demand greater transparency and stronger evidence from a government than from a private citizen. Power carries responsibilities, and whoever controls information must be accountable for it.

The problem is not that asymmetry. The problem arises when, while demanding that the government justify every last detail of its version, we allow ourselves to assert any alternative explanation having demanded practically no evidence of ourselves.

**It is not about demanding of ourselves the same as of the government, but about demanding something.**

We may have excellent reasons to distrust an official explanation and still not know what really happened. A government's failure to sufficiently prove its version does not mean we have proved ours.

The curious thing is that those who fall into this behaviour may consider themselves especially sceptical. They distrust governments, the media and large institutions.

However, distrusting a source does not automatically make us critical thinkers.

We can uncritically reject one version and just as uncritically accept its opposite.

We haven't replaced credulity with scepticism. We have simply changed the object of our credulity.

And this double standard becomes particularly dangerous when our political preferences come into play.

Consider the recent death of Maricarmen Abascal, an 87-year-old woman who was evicted from the Madrid home where she had lived for seven decades. After being evicted on 23 September, she was taken to the Gregorio Marañón Hospital, where she died on 7 October. Her case sparked an enormous social mobilisation and became a political issue of the first order.

After her death became known, messages began to circulate insinuating, without providing any evidence, that the PSOE or people linked to it might have deliberately caused it to further their electoral interests.

The reasoning is revealing: her death could benefit the PSOE politically; therefore, someone in the PSOE could be behind it.

But since when does potentially benefiting from an event prove having caused it?

I can disagree profoundly with Pedro Sánchez and his policies. I can consider that his government bears political responsibilities for which it must be held to account. None of those positions entitles me to attribute a crime to Sánchez or his party without evidence.

**And refusing to do so does not mean defending him.**

It means that my judgement about the facts should not depend on how much I like or dislike the person involved.

It is one thing to discuss the political responsibilities surrounding an eviction. Another to examine the possible consequences it had for a person's health. And something completely different to accuse someone of having deliberately caused her death.

If we only demand evidence when the accusation harms those we care about, we are not defending the truth. We are defending our own side.

## A country of know-it-alls

There is another problem related to all this, perhaps even more widespread: we have come to think that we need to have an opinion on any subject.

In Spain we know the figure of the *expert on everything* very well. He is that talk-show pundit who on Monday explains the causes of an economic crisis, on Tuesday analyses a military operation, on Wednesday interprets a court ruling and on Thursday reveals to us the keys to a scientific breakthrough.

It doesn't much matter what the subject is. He always seems to have a formed opinion and generally expresses it with admirable certainty.

The figure is so recognisable that it almost seems like a comic character. But behind the caricature lies a fairly serious question: at what point did we begin to confuse the ability to give an opinion on anything with intelligence?

It is not a new criticism. Carlos Taibo already addressed this figure in *Contra los tertulianos*, originally published in 2010 and expanded in 2021. More recently, the journalist Antonio Villarreal has examined it again in his book *Tertulianos. Un viaje a la industria de la opinión en España*.

A particularly revealing testimony is that of the journalist María Jesús Güemes, a former panellist, who recalled how, during a debate on a fire at a tyre plant, her fellow panellists hurriedly searched for information on their devices to improvise arguments. She herself admitted that she was able to contribute next to nothing of value and that it would have been necessary to bring in a specialist.

Not all pundits behave like this, naturally. Nor is it illegitimate for a cultured person to take an interest in subjects outside their speciality.

The problem is unjustified certainty.

An economist may not know the causes of an epidemic. A doctor may not know how to interpret a geopolitical conflict. And a citizen interested in current affairs may lack enough information to take a position on a legal controversy.

None of that makes them less intelligent.

Perhaps what should surprise us is not that someone acknowledges their ignorance, but that there are people who never seem to feel the need to.

This culture also seeps into our conversations.

Imagine a gathering of friends at which someone puts forward a theory about an international conflict. One offers a geopolitical explanation. Another brings in economic interests. A third links the event to decisions taken years earlier.

They all seem to understand perfectly what has happened.

And then someone says: "Well, I don't have enough information to know what happened."

That person may be precisely the most rigorous one in the group.

Yet they run the risk of seeming the least interesting, the least informed or the one who has thought about it least.

We have turned having an opinion into a kind of social obligation. Admitting that we don't know something can be interpreted as a weakness, as if it meant that we have no judgement or that we haven't bothered to think.

Talk shows and social media can reinforce this behaviour. A forceful statement is usually easier to turn into a headline than an explanation full of nuance. Certainty fits in a sentence; justifying uncertainty usually takes several paragraphs.

And so we can end up rewarding not the person who best distinguishes what they know from what they don't, but the one who speaks with the greatest conviction.

## The question we almost never ask ourselves

Faced with a surprising claim, there is a very simple question that can be especially useful:

**And how do you know?**

It is not the same as asking "why do you think that?". The latter can lead the other person to explain their theory all over again. The former points to the evidence that justifies their degree of certainty.

But there is a second question that I find even more interesting:

**What piece of information would make you change your mind?**

Imagine that someone maintains that a particular international crisis was provoked by another country's secret services.

What information could lead them to abandon that hypothesis?

If contrary evidence emerged, would they examine it? Or would they immediately find an explanation for dismissing it?

If the absence of documents proves that the operation was secret, contradictory testimony proves that the witnesses have been manipulated and any denial constitutes confirmation of the cover-up, what real chance is there that the theory is false?

An explanation that can accommodate any outcome may seem very powerful. But it may also have become immune to testing.

That does not mean that every hypothesis must be capable of being ruled out by a single simple piece of evidence. Historical and political events are often complex. What matters is maintaining a genuine willingness to revise our conclusions when new evidence appears.

And, of course, we shouldn't direct this question only at others.

We should also direct it at ourselves.

What would I have to discover to abandon an opinion I have defended for years? Am I willing to accept a conclusion that contradicts my political preferences? Am I looking for evidence to find out what happened, or for arguments to prove that I was right?

These are uncomfortable questions.

That is precisely why they are worth asking.

## Socrates had already identified the problem

Some 2,400 years ago, Socrates put forward a reflection that seems written for our times.

We usually sum it up with the famous phrase "I only know that I know nothing", although that is not its literal wording in the texts that have come down to us.

In the *Apology of Socrates*, Plato recounts how the philosopher examined a man who enjoyed a reputation for wisdom. After talking with him, he concluded that neither of them possessed certain kinds of knowledge. The difference was that the other man believed he knew what he did not.

Socrates, by contrast, acknowledged his lack of knowledge.

His conclusion can be expressed like this:

**"What I do not know, I do not think I know either."**

It seems to me one of the most valuable ideas in all of philosophy.

Not because we should resign ourselves to ignorance, nor because it is impossible to attain solid knowledge, but because recognising the limits of our knowledge is a condition for continuing to learn.

If we already think we know the answer, why investigate?

If an explanation satisfies us completely, what incentive do we have to look for evidence that might disprove it?

Socratic wisdom does not consist in boasting of ignorance. It consists in not disguising it as knowledge.

## It's not about believing the official versions

Something important needs to be made clear.

None of the above means that we should automatically accept the explanations of governments, the media or institutions.

Conspiracies exist. Governments lie. Secret operations are carried out. And some hypotheses that initially seemed far-fetched have turned out to be true.

Nor do we need a signed confession to reach reasonable conclusions. Circumstantial evidence, when it is solid and correctly analysed, can constitute important proof.

But precisely because real conspiracies exist, we should take seriously the task of distinguishing them from imaginary ones.

It is not enough to list events compatible with our theory. We must ask ourselves whether those events would also be compatible with other explanations, and what evidence would allow us to distinguish between them.

**Intellectual rigour does not consist in rejecting what has not yet been proved. It consists in not presenting it as proved before its time.**

## The discipline of recognising that we can be wrong

There is one last danger I don't want to overlook: turning intellectual rigour into a new excuse to feel superior to others.

It would be rather contradictory to denounce those who enjoy believing themselves sharper while we congratulate ourselves on being much more rigorous than they are.

We all have prejudices. We are all prone to interpreting events in a way that favours our ideas. And we can all fall in love with an explanation because it is particularly clever or because it confirms something we wanted to believe.

Me too.

That is precisely why we need criteria that do not depend on our preferences.

Even being right does not necessarily prove that we have reasoned well. We can arrive at a true conclusion for completely wrong reasons.

**Being right by chance is not the same as having reasoned correctly.**

Perhaps we should value less the ability to offer instant explanations and more the willingness to subject them to scrutiny.

We don't need to have an opinion on everything. We don't need to find a culprit for every misfortune or a conspiracy behind every coincidence. And much less do we need to keep proving that we can see what others cannot.

Sometimes we will have enough evidence to take a firm position. At other times we will only have suspicions. And there will be occasions when we simply won't know the answer.

And that should not make us ashamed.

Because perhaps one of the greatest displays of intelligence does not lie in always having something to say, but in knowing when it is time to utter three words:

**I don't know.**
